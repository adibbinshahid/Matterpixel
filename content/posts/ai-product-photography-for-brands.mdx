AI product photography has moved quickly from novelty to a practical tool. For many brands it now produces lifestyle and campaign images that would otherwise need a studio, a set, a stylist and a photographer. It also still gets some things badly wrong, and the brands that use it well are the ones that know exactly where that line is.

This guide covers what AI product photography is, the shots it does well, where it fails, the rules you need to respect on marketplaces and in copyright, how much it costs compared with a traditional shoot, and how to brief a set that actually sells.

## What is AI product photography?

There are two quite different things sold under this name, and the difference matters.

**Fully generated images** are created from a text prompt alone. The model invents the product along with everything else. These are useful for mood boards and concept work, but the product in the image is not your product. It will have the wrong proportions, an invented label and details that do not exist.

**AI-composited images** start from a real photograph of your actual product, usually a clean packshot. The AI builds the scene around it: the surface, the background, the lighting, props, reflections and shadows. The product stays true to life because it is a photograph of the real thing.

For anything a customer will buy from, only the second approach is acceptable. Whichever workflow is used, nothing should ship raw: every image we deliver goes through manual review and correction first.

## What it does well

### Lifestyle and context shots

The biggest win is placing a product in an environment: a skincare jar on a marble bathroom shelf in morning light, a bottle on a picnic blanket, a watch on a wrist at a café table. Traditionally each scene needs a location or a built set. With AI compositing, a brand can produce a dozen different settings from one packshot.

### Seasonal and campaign variations

The same product can be shown in a winter scene for December, a beach setting for summer and a gifting set-up for a holiday sale, without reshooting. That makes it much easier to keep social channels and ads fresh.

### Consistent sets across a range

Brands with many products often struggle to make their catalogue look consistent when it was shot at different times by different people. AI compositing can place an entire range into the same lighting and setting, so the collection reads as one brand.

### Speed

A traditional shoot needs planning, booking, shooting and retouching. A well-briefed AI set can move from brief to finished images in days rather than weeks, which matters for launches and promotions.

## Where it still fails

Being honest about the weaknesses is what separates a set that sells from a folder of near-misses.

- **Text and labels.** AI models still distort small print, logos and ingredient lists if they are allowed to redraw them. The product label must come from the real photo, untouched.
- **Fine detail and materials.** Jewellery settings, fabric weave, stitching, transparent glass and liquids are easy to get subtly wrong. They need close checking at full size.
- **Hands and people.** Products held in hands, or worn by models, are improving but still produce errors in fingers, grip and scale. These shots need the most review.
- **Scale and physics.** A product can end up the wrong size relative to the props around it, or cast a shadow in the wrong direction. Small errors like these make an image feel fake even when the viewer cannot say why.
- **Exact colour.** Colour accuracy matters for fashion, cosmetics and home goods, where a wrong shade leads to returns. Colours should be checked against the physical product.

The practical rule: AI is excellent for the scene, and should never be trusted with the product itself.

## Marketplace rules you must follow

Marketplaces care about one thing above all: that the image shows what the customer will actually receive.

**Amazon**, for example, requires the main product image to be on a pure white background, with the product filling most of the frame, and prohibits images that misrepresent the product. Lifestyle images belong in the secondary image slots. AI-composited lifestyle shots work well there; a fully generated "product" does not belong anywhere on a listing.

The same principle applies everywhere you sell. Using images that make a product look different from the real thing is not just against marketplace policies. In many countries it can also fall foul of consumer protection and advertising rules, and it reliably increases returns and bad reviews.

## Copyright and ownership

This is the question most brands forget to ask. The United States Copyright Office published guidance in January 2025 on the copyrightability of AI-generated outputs. Its position, in short, is that material generated entirely by AI is not protected by copyright, while works that include sufficient human creative contribution can be protected for those human elements. Prompts alone are generally not enough.

What this means in practice:

- Your **real product photograph** and your **product design and branding** remain yours.
- The **arrangement, selection, editing and retouching** done by people can add protectable human authorship.
- A purely generated background may not be something you can stop others from copying.

Rules differ between countries and are still developing, so for high-value campaigns it is worth taking advice. At minimum, make sure your contract with any agency transfers all rights it holds in the final images to you.

## What it costs compared with a traditional shoot

A traditional lifestyle shoot involves a photographer's day rate, a studio or location, props, styling, possibly models, and retouching per image. Costs vary enormously by market, but even a modest shoot quickly reaches four figures before a single image is delivered, and every new scene means another shoot.

AI-composited sets are usually priced per set of finished images. The real costs are the clean starting photographs, the art direction and the human review and retouching, not the generation itself. ## How to brief an AI product shoot

A good brief makes the difference between a set that ships and a folder of near-misses. Include:

1. **Clean source photos.** Sharp, evenly lit photographs of the real product from the angles you need, on a plain background. This is the single biggest factor in quality.
2. **Where the images will be used.** Website hero, marketplace secondary images, Instagram feed, Stories and ads all need different crops and compositions.
3. **Your customer and setting.** Who buys this, and where would they use it? A busy parent's kitchen and a luxury hotel bathroom are very different scenes.
4. **References.** A few images you like and a few you do not. This saves more time than any written description.
5. **Brand rules.** Colours, mood, props to avoid, and anything legally required to appear on pack.
6. **The non-negotiables.** Exact colour accuracy, readable labels, correct scale, and who signs off before anything is published.

## What to ask before you hire a provider

1. **Ask to see raw output next to the delivered image.** If a provider cannot show you the correction step, there probably is not one.
2. **Judge a full set, not one hero shot.** A single great image is easy. Twenty consistent ones is the actual skill.
3. **Compare turnaround with a real shoot.** The value is speed and quality together. If it is not meaningfully faster than a traditional shoot, the AI step is not earning its keep.
4. **Check who owns the images.** The contract should transfer every right the provider holds in the final images to you.

## How to tell if a set is good enough to publish

Before using any AI image, check it at full size against the real product:

- Is the label identical to the real packaging?
- Is the colour right under neutral light?
- Is the product the right size relative to its surroundings?
- Do the shadows and reflections fall in a consistent direction?
- Are there any distorted edges, extra parts or merged objects?
- Would a customer who received this product feel misled by the image?

If the answer to the last question is anything other than a clear no, do not use it.

## Is AI product photography right for your brand?

It is a strong fit if you sell physical products, need a steady flow of lifestyle and campaign imagery, and want consistency across a range. It is a poor fit for anything where the product itself must be shown with absolute precision, such as fine jewellery close-ups or technical parts, where a skilled photographer is still the better choice.
