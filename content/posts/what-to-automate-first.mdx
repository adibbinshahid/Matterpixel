"Add AI" is not a plan. Every automation we scope starts with a simpler question: where does your team's time go every week, and which of those hours don't need a human judgment call?

## Start with the slow reply, not the clever feature

The most expensive gap in a small business is rarely a missing feature. It's the time between a customer asking something and someone answering. A lead that waits four hours for a reply has often already asked someone else.

That makes first contact the best place to start: an assistant that answers common questions on WhatsApp or your site, collects the details you'd ask for anyway, and books the call straight into your calendar. It doesn't replace the conversation. It makes sure the conversation happens while the customer still cares.

## Then remove the copy-paste

The second place to look is any task where someone moves the same information between two tools. Typical examples:

- An order arrives by email and gets retyped into a spreadsheet.
- A booking is confirmed, then a reminder is sent by hand the day before.
- A new lead is added to a CRM, then a follow-up is set by memory.

Each of these is a fixed path with no decision in the middle. That's what workflows are good at, and it's where the hours come back first.

## What should stay with a person

Automation works when the rule is clear. It breaks where judgment is the point:

1. **Pricing and exceptions.** A bot can collect the brief. A person should set the number.
2. **Complaints.** Route them fast, but answer them yourself.
3. **Anything you can't explain as a rule.** If you can't write down how you decide, the system can't either.

## How to tell if it's worth building

Before anything gets built, three numbers should be on the table: how often the task happens, how long it takes each time, and what a mistake costs. A task that happens twice a month isn't worth a system. A task that eats an hour every day usually is.

That's the order we scope in: reply speed first, copy-paste second, and judgment left where it belongs. The result is fewer tools, not more, and a team that spends its time on the work only it can do.
